After helping launch the aluminum-bodied [[F-150]] at Dearborn Stamping, I was sent to the Buffalo Stamping Plant for my next major assignment: serving as the launch lead for the stamping side of the MY16 Ford Edge and Lincoln MKX program.

At that point in my career, it felt like a big step. Dearborn had already taught me what a major launch looked like when the whole company was watching. Buffalo was different. It was a new setting, a new team, and a new kind of responsibility. I was no longer just supporting a launch effort inside a larger machine. I was much closer to the center of it, helping lead the work, absorb the pressure, and keep the program moving when real production problems started showing up.

![[image-40.png]]

The Buffalo Stamping Plant had its own personality. It felt like a true industrial town plant: proud, gritty, weathered, and full of people who knew how to make things happen. Buffalo itself left an impression on me too. I still remember the long drives, the snow, the gray mornings, and the feeling of being away from home for so much of that year. I was constantly commuting between Michigan and Buffalo, living out of a hotel room, waking up in the dark, and preparing for another day where every issue seemed urgent. By the end of that stretch, I had spent more than 230 nights in a hotel. I do not think I will ever break that record again, and honestly, I would not want to.

Even so, there was something meaningful about that period. When you are dropped into a plant in the middle of a launch, with the clock running and the problems coming at you daily, life becomes very simple in one sense. You wake up, go to the plant, work the issues, align the team, and try to leave things in a better state than you found them. Then you do it again the next day.

The MY16 Edge and MKX program was an important one for Ford. Buffalo had recently installed a new high-speed stamping line, and with that came the promise of higher output along with a completely new set of headaches. On paper, high-speed automation and new equipment are exciting. In reality, launching them at production pace can be humbling. The system had to be able to run fast, consistently, and reliably, not just once, but day after day under the messiness of real manufacturing.

That was one of the hardest parts of the job: balancing risk against output. You had to push the line hard enough to learn something real about the process, but not so hard that you created new instability, major downtime, or unnecessary scrap. There was always a tension between the short-term need to keep production moving and the longer-term need to solve the process the right way. In launch work, that tension never really goes away. You are always making judgment calls with incomplete information, trying to decide what needs immediate containment and what needs deeper correction.

The pace of the work made that even more intense. Every morning started early. We met at 6:00 a.m. for executive reviews with our director, covering the key issues from the previous shift, the quality problems still open, the production risks for the day, and the volume numbers everyone cared about. Those meetings set the tone. By that hour, everyone was already in problem-solving mode. There was very little room for vagueness. You had to know what the issues were, who owned them, what actions were underway, and what the realistic path forward looked like.

That rhythm taught me a lot. It taught me how important clarity is when pressure is high. It taught me that people do not need drama from a leader when a plant is struggling. They need honesty, calm, and direction. They need someone who can say, “Here is the issue, here is what we know, here is what we do not know, and here is what we are doing next.”

![[image-31.png]]

A big part of the launch involved solving the production issues that naturally come with a new stamping program. We were dealing with splits, contamination, surface defects, and wavy finishes. None of those were abstract engineering problems. They affected whether the part could run, whether quality could hold, and whether the plant could build enough volume with enough confidence to keep the launch on track.

Some of those problems were similar to what I had already seen on the F-150, but each plant and each program has its own character. In Buffalo, the technical challenge was shaped not just by the part design or die conditions, but by the interaction between new equipment, local process realities, plant habits, supplier systems, and the speed at which everything was expected to come together. That is one of the things I learned early in manufacturing: no issue ever exists in isolation. Even a defect that looks simple on the surface is usually tied into a whole web of conditions underneath.

One of the most memorable technical areas I got involved with was an ABB robotic blank loading system that used a new ABB vision system for blank positioning. On paper, this may have sounded like a side issue relative to the bigger launch problems, but in reality it became one of the more formative parts of my time there.

![[image-41.png]]

My formal role was launch lead, not controls engineer or robotics programmer. But I have always had a hard time standing at the edge of a technical problem if I think I can learn my way into it. Rather than depending entirely on the supplier to diagnose and fix every issue, I decided to dig in and learn how the vision system worked myself. I wanted to understand how the logic was built, how the camera interpreted blank location, how offsets were handled, and what might be causing inconsistency at the robot loading cell.

That decision made a real difference. Once I got closer to the details, the system became less mysterious. I could speak more clearly with the supplier, ask better questions, and help move the team toward practical fixes faster. More importantly, it gave me confidence. It was one of my first truly hands-on experiences with robotics in a high-speed manufacturing environment, and I remember how satisfying it felt to not just coordinate around the issue, but to actually understand part of the underlying technology myself.

That experience stayed with me long after the launch. It changed the way I thought about engineering leadership. A leader does not need to be the deepest technical expert in every domain, but there is tremendous value in being willing to cross boundaries, learn unfamiliar systems, and get close enough to the work to build real credibility. Sometimes the biggest contribution you can make is simply refusing to leave the technical problem entirely in someone else’s hands.

The launch itself was a grind. Days blurred together. Some issues seemed to resolve, only to reappear under slightly different conditions. You would think you had stabilized one part of the process, then a new variable would show up: contamination, a surface issue, an inconsistency at speed, a tooling-related concern, or a downstream quality reaction. It required constant follow-up and a willingness to live inside the details without losing sight of the broader mission.

That was where the human side of the job became just as important as the technical side. As launch lead, I was often acting as a bridge between headquarters and the local Buffalo team. That meant carrying expectations in both directions. From one side came urgency, timing pressure, and the need for clear status. From the other side came the reality of what the plant was actually dealing with moment to moment. I had to help translate those worlds to each other in a way that built trust instead of friction.

That role taught me a lot about alignment. It is easy in a company to imagine that everyone is looking at the same problem the same way. In reality, each group sees a different slice of it. Plant teams feel the physical and operational pain of the issue. Program teams feel the milestone pressure. Engineering teams see the technical cause-and-effect. Leadership sees the business risk. One of the hardest and most important jobs in a launch is helping those perspectives come together into shared action.

I also worked on forklift optimization while I was there, making sure we had the right support equipment and flow strategy to keep production moving. That may sound like a smaller operational detail compared with robotics or stamping quality, but in a plant environment those details matter a great deal. Throughput is never just about whether the press runs. It depends on whether the entire surrounding system works: material delivery, part movement, labor balance, support equipment, and the countless handoffs between processes.

That work also gave me an opportunity to validate some of my [[MODAPTS]] thinking in a real production environment. It reinforced something I came to believe strongly over time: if you want to understand a manufacturing system, you cannot just study the glamorous part of it. You have to study the whole chain. Often the limiting factor is not the machine everyone is paying attention to, but some secondary process that quietly creates drag on everything else.

What I remember most, though, is the people.

The team in Buffalo made a real difference in my life. I admired many of the people I worked with there, both for their toughness and for their willingness to keep showing up and solving problems under pressure. There is a kind of character you meet in plants like that, especially in places shaped by weather, industrial history, and hard work. People tend to be direct. They tend to know what matters. They do not have much patience for theater. I appreciated that.

There was also something about being away from home, in a cold industrial city, working long days with the same group of people, that created a kind of closeness. You saw people tired, frustrated, determined, funny, sharp, and resilient, often all in the same day. Launches strip away a lot of pretense. Over time, you get to know who stays steady, who brings solutions, who lifts the room, and who can be counted on when the pressure rises.

Looking back, Buffalo was one of those assignments that changed me not because everything went smoothly, but because it did not. It forced me to grow. It pushed me further into leadership, deeper into technical ownership, and closer to the messy reality of how manufacturing problems are actually solved. It taught me how to navigate tension between urgency and patience, between headquarters and the plant, and between trying to be decisive while still learning in real time.

Most of all, it taught me that good launch leadership is not about standing above the work. It is about getting inside it. It is about knowing when to escalate, when to listen, when to push, when to stabilize, and when to roll up your sleeves and learn something outside your formal job description because the team needs it.

When I think back on that year, I do not just remember the issues, the meetings, or the endless hotel nights. I remember the feeling of being in motion all the time, of being stretched by the work, and of slowly becoming more capable because the situation demanded it. Buffalo was hard. It was cold. It was exhausting. But it was also one of the assignments that most clearly shaped the engineer and leader I later became.
