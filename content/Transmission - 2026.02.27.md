### 1. Improved Paramount bid for Warner Bros

Paramount increased their bid for [Warner Bros. Discovery (WBD)](https://www.google.com/search?client=firefox-b-1-d&q=Warner+Bros.+Discovery+%28WBD%29&ved=2ahUKEwiWqI_x0_iSAxXNMNAFHbI5ECoQgK4QegQIARAB) to \$31 per share in an all-cash deal, surpassing the previous offer and prompting the WBD board to deem it a superior proposal. This enhanced offer includes a \$7 billion regulatory termination fee and coverage of the \$2.8 billion fee owed to Netflix for breaking their prior agreement. 

One interesting aspect of this deal is the *regulatory protection* and the "time bomb" fee if the deal is not closed on time. The time bomb is called a ticking fee at \$0.25 per share per quarter if the deal doesn't close after September 30, 2026. This seems like a signal to the shareholder that they have the regulators on Paramount's side, which they probably do with the political coverage they will get from the currently administration.

### 2. Nvidia Continues to Deliver Strong Earnings

Selling shovels in a gold rush continues to be the right play. Nvidia reported a net income of \$43 billion, up \$22 billion from just a year ago. What's even crazier is how they did it on just \$68.1 billion of sales. This is really amazing profit margin for a hardware based company, but when you have the technology advantage, you can basically charge whatever you want.

I spent some time learning about GPU vs. CPU vs. TPU. The currently focus in [[AI]] has been dominated by *training*, but *inference* seems to growing fast. [[Inference]] is the use of AI on practical applications, which to me signals that these models are getting so good, we are now focusing on how to use them instead of just pumping out better models. But the race won't slow down any time soon.

### 3. Supreme Court Found IEEPA to be Illegal

On February 20, 2026, the [Supreme Court](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1287_4gcj.pdf) ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the president to impose sweeping, open-ended tariffs. Right after, the President added broad 10% tariff then increased it to 15%. He now has less flexibility to impose targeting tariffs without going through congress.

Before this, many countries worked with the U.S. government to negotiate specific deals, in an attempt to reduce their tariff requirements. The UK was the first and many followed. Notably, China slow played this and now benefitted from not rushing to make a deal with the US. 

During an interview on the [[All In podcast]], the secretary of commerce, Howard Lutnick talked extensively about nations losing out due to first mover advantage. Now it seems that waiting was the right strategy.

Another interesting aspect to this is refunds. If the tariffs are indeed illegal, companies that paid into the tariff might be qualified to sue for refunds. But that would risk offending the administration, which is not ideal for most companies. In some cases, companies like Toyota lost over \$6 billions due to tariff, this is no small number.
