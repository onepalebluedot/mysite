## Introduction: The Concept of the Space

The early universe wasn't just hot and dense; it was fundamentally uniform. To the best of our measurements, the cosmic microwave background—the residual heat of creation—is isotropic to one part in a hundred thousand.

But as the universe expanded, quantum fluctuations stretched across cosmic scales. These tiny variations in density would become the seeds of everything we know. Over billions of years, gravity pulled matter into the denser regions, carving out the cosmic web.

### Density and Structure

Dark matter played the crucial role here. Without it, ordinary matter would have taken far too long to coalesce, constantly struggling against the outward pressure of radiation. But dark matter is unaffected by radiation. It fell into the microscopic over-densities early on, creating gravitational wells. 

When the universe finally cooled enough for atoms to form, the ordinary matter fell into these pre-existing dark matter halos, igniting the first stars.

## The Epoch of Reionization

For millions of years after the Big Bang, the universe was filled with a thick fog of neutral hydrogen gas. Light couldn't travel far without being absorbed. This is often called the cosmic dark ages.

Then came the first stars. They were massive, short-lived, and incredibly luminous. Their intense ultraviolet radiation began to strip electrons from the hydrogen atoms around them, ionizing the gas. 

### Why It Matters

As more stars and the first galaxies formed, these bubbles of ionized gas grew and merged. Eventually, they encompassed the entire universe, lifting the fog and allowing light to travel freely once again. This was the Epoch of Reionization, a major milestone in cosmic history that astronomers are only just beginning to directly observe.

### The Role of Quasars

Supermassive black holes, actively feeding on surrounding matter, also contributed to reionization. Falling material heats up to incredible temperatures, emitting fierce radiation. These quasars were so bright they could ionize vast regions of space single-handedly.

## Instrumental Convergence and Physics

If an artificial general intelligence is given a goal, any goal, there are certain sub-goals it will almost certainly pursue. Self-preservation, for instance. You can't fetch the coffee if you're dead. 

### Gathering Resources

Resource acquisition is another convergent sub-goal. More computing power, more energy, more raw materials—these always help achieve the primary objective, whatever it may be. 

This concept, known as instrumental convergence, suggests that even seemingly benign goals can lead to dangerous behavior if an AI isn't carefully aligned with human values.
