During my time as an industrial engineer at Ford, I had the opportunity to work on one of the most important vehicle programs in the company’s history: the launch of the first all-aluminum Ford [[F-150]] in 2014 and 2015. The F-150 had already been the best-selling truck in the United States for many years, so this program carried enormous weight for the business. It was not simply a new model launch. It represented a major shift in how Ford designed and built its highest-profile product. Before this program, no other mass-produced vehicle had an all-aluminum body at this scale. For Ford stamping operations, it was one of the biggest manufacturing challenges in years.

![[image-28.png]]

The move to an aluminum-intensive body was a first for the F-150 and a major manufacturing challenge. It required new thinking across stamping, assembly, quality, and launch operations. I was based at the Dearborn Stamping Plant, where my role centered on managing quality issues, coordinating problem-solving efforts, and bringing together the right stakeholders to address risks quickly. The work demanded constant collaboration across manufacturing engineering, plant operations, die engineering, process engineering, quality, and the UAW-represented production team.

Because this was one of the company’s most important programs, the plant was full of people working with urgency and purpose. Everyone understood what was at stake. At the same time, when large and diverse teams come together under pressure, differences of opinion are inevitable. That was when true [[Leadership Principles|leadership]] mattered most: creating alignment, keeping people focused, and helping the organization move forward.

As a young and impressionable engineer, I got to see what that kind of leadership looked like up close, both from colleagues and from the broader leadership team. When the program encountered technical challenges that many of us had never seen before, I watched experienced leaders create momentum by bringing the right people together, staying calm under pressure, and keeping the focus on solving the problem rather than assigning blame.

![[image-32.png]]

What made the program so significant was the combination of scale and novelty. The F-150 was critical to Ford’s performance, and at the same time we were dealing with manufacturing conditions that were new to many of us. Aluminum behaves differently from traditional automotive steel, especially in high-volume stamping operations. That meant many of the assumptions and instincts that had worked on earlier programs had to be revisited with much greater care.

#### Technical Challenge

One recurring challenge we faced was splits in stamped parts. In simple terms, a split happens when the sheet is stretched beyond what it can sustain, causing local thinning to accelerate until the material tears. In production, the crack is what everyone sees, but scientifically it is really the final symptom of strain becoming too concentrated in one area of the part.

This became especially important on the aluminum-bodied F-150. In stamping, the sheet is constantly being asked to flow, draw in, and stretch in a controlled way. If one region is over-restrained by binder pressure, draw beads, friction, geometry, or poor material feed, that area can begin to thin much faster than the rest. Once that happens, the remaining cross section has less ability to carry load, and failure can occur quickly. Aluminum made this even more challenging because it was often less forgiving than the steels many teams were more familiar with. In practical terms, it could reach critical thinning sooner and give us a narrower process window to work with.

A useful way to think about it is through strain and thinning. As the material stretches, its thickness must reduce. In simplified form:

$$
\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 + \varepsilon_3 = 0
$$

where $\varepsilon_1$ and $\varepsilon_2$ are the in-plane strains and $\varepsilon_3$ is the thickness strain. If both in-plane strains become highly positive, thickness drops rapidly. That is why split-prone areas often appear in corners, radii, walls, and other regions where the die is forcing severe local stretch.

I worked closely with the die engineering team to review simulation data, compare it with what we were seeing in production trials, and run experiments to reduce risk and improve part quality. Simulation was extremely useful because it helped identify likely hot spots for thinning and failure before or during tool trials. But real production never behaves exactly like the model. Lubrication changes, coil-to-coil variation, die condition, press behavior, and other shop-floor realities all influence whether a marginal area becomes a true split problem. That meant we had to constantly connect the digital analysis back to physical trials, measured data, and what the operators and engineers were actually seeing on the floor.

That cycle of simulation, testing, observation, and adjustment was one of the most valuable learning experiences of the program. It taught me that manufacturing problems are rarely solved by theory alone. The real work is in bridging the gap between what should happen mathematically and what actually happens in production. For a deeper explanation of the science behind splits, material behavior, and why aluminum can be so different from steel, I wrote a separate [[Why Splits Happen in Stamping — and Why Aluminum Behaves Differently Than Steel|article]].

I supported launch readiness from the stamping side, where the pressure was relentless. Every issue mattered because small process problems could quickly become major quality or throughput problems. My job was to help identify root causes, align the people who owned different parts of the process, and keep the team moving toward solutions while staying on schedule. Because this was such a visible program, the standard for execution was extremely high.

![[image-29.png]]

Another key part of my role was creating and working on [[MODAPTS]] studies to analyze operator work content. We needed to make sure we had the right number of people assigned to each job while also protecting safety and ergonomics. On a launch program, labor balance is not just a staffing question. It directly affects throughput, quality, fatigue, and the team’s ability to execute consistently under pressure. That work required careful observation, disciplined time study, and close coordination with production leadership and UAW stakeholders.

![[image-33.png]]

I also gained a much deeper appreciation for how much launch work depends on teamwork. No one solved these problems alone. Success came from getting the right people in the room, clarifying the problem, testing ideas quickly, and keeping everyone aligned when the timeline was tight. That environment pushed me to grow not only as a technical engineer, but also as a communicator, facilitator, and teammate.

Looking back, the launch was one of the most challenging periods of my early career. We were trying to hit aggressive milestones while solving manufacturing problems we had never seen before at that scale. It was demanding, but it was also one of the most formative experiences I have had. The program taught me how to work under pressure, how to navigate competing priorities, and how to help teams solve hard problems in real time.

![[image-30.png]]

That experience directly set me up for my next major assignment: going to the Buffalo Stamping Plant to support the launch of the [[Ford Edge and Lincoln MkX|Ford Edge and Lincoln MKX program]] as the launch leader the following year. The F-150 program gave me the technical foundation and the team-based problem-solving mindset that I carried into that role.
