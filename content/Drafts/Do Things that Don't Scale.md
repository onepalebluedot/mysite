"Startups take off because the founders make them take off". This idea says a lot about why many startup owners stay in charge way after a business takes off. Idea is simple, but execution is hard, and founders are the executions. A good metaphor is "engine crank" on a car. 

Recruit users manually. Engineers are not used to being in charge and they want to avoid the sales. In fact, the important thing is to scale by percentage. Say 10% growth a year, you start at 100 and in 2 years you can be at 2 million users. [[Scale Brain]]

**Lesson from Stripe:** Instead of sending a potential customer a link after the meeting, ask for the laptop and get them set up right away. 

People dismissing your startup is harmless. When they see growth they will change.

**Treating the first customers well:** Make sure you take care of the first users. At Ford, we are used to being the ones being pampered, instead we need to turn that around and make sure everyone involved feel appreciated. Handwritten notes are a good idea. 

Other startups might be more open to new ideas.

Hardware startup: Facing a different problem because it requires investment and convincing someone to be invest in it. You can tweak the design faster when you are the factory. **Key is that you should know your product well and control it.** For B2B, be extreme about serving your customer. Customize as much as you can then other's will want it too. 

Partnership with a big company usually doesn't work. But they should be your customer. Google was the customer of Yahoo, not partner and that helped them grow. 

### Takeaways:
- In the case for [[AIMS]], it is currently a hardware focused company with B2B as the focus. So, finding an early customer is paramount. Who would it be? Is it the integrator or the end user? I think it makes to find an user any way possible If it's an integrator, so be it, if not, then let's find someone else. After that, we will need to be extreme about serving them, because their success is your success. 
- But how do you sell them the product when you don't have it? Or when you can't sell it? Pre-order? Perhaps, then we need to set a strong deadline to deliver, at any cost. Let's say [[walmart]] wants to use it, we a pre-order of x number of units. We need to work diligently to deliver the most amazing experience with the best results. From what we learned during IT PC renew, it's not actually how bad their process is, but rather that we didn't work hard enough to deliver that pilot and ensure it become as successful as possible, including doing something that we are not good at. We did some of that, but not enough. If Walmart is the customer, I think I'd have an easier time convincing Ford to put some resources toward it.
- For the matter proving that the system could cost less. DFM ([[Design for Manufacturing]]) through a trusted source might be the way to go. By saving we've done it with cost estimating might not be good enough, but we actually need to show results. If that's not good enough, maybe we ask for resources to validate it through a design firm that they trust. In no way we can actually product something for the final cost unless we invest money into it. The comprehensive cost model is something we need to present.
